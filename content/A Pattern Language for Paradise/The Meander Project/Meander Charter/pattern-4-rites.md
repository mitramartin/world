A joke among Meander’s early founders was that rituals were their love language. Many, including us, truly believed that well-held rituals would support raiment wellbeing in many ways, and encourage motivation for complex entanglements and such.

At the same time, one of the most difficult themes that we have engaged related to rites-of-passage. Deliberations were fraught with concern about cultural appropriation, arbitrariness, safety, power literacy, guruism, etc. Youth-focused rituals were the center of many General Circle dialogues in the early years. We reinforced a very intentional framing of rites as experiments; however, the rituals themselves were certainly infused with a potency that did not feel experimental to the children who underwent them. 

In designing the rites, a primary goal was inviting the child into an ever deeper and wider (yet developmentally appropriate) sense of responsibility and agency. What we did not realize was how frequently this would put the child and her evolving mandate at odds with the conventional beliefs of their parents’ generation. 

For example, most first-generation Meander parents were oriented toward isolationism. But for many of their children, the early rite-of-passage experiences connected them with a thirst to entangle closely with their Alexandria neighbors, which certainly generated much friction. Now we can see this as a natural progression of the pan-raiment project.
