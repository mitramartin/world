**What is onboarding

Onboarding is welcoming and orienting someone into a context that’s new to them. Onboard! It helps them complete the process of *entering* so they can get on with what they want to do in the new place. 

The place could be an actual literal place, like a boat or an airplane or a spaceship. Or a campus or a monastery or a country like Switzerland, or the new company that they were just hired at. Or it could be a metaphorical place, like a domain of practice, such as cooking, or parenting, or dancing the tango. 

Or, important for our purposes, it could be a tool space. A medium that requires us to use our bodies in particular, specific ways is generally experienced by humans as an immersive kind of ecology, i.e., a place. So, a piece of technology like a car, a television, a smartphone, or even a specific software application also requires helping someone onboard, which is to say an onboarding process.  

In any of these cases, a comprehensive onboarding will not only welcome the newcomer and ensure they do not hurt themselves while they are trying to get on, it will also give them a minimum viable amount of knowledge to start functioning, and provide guardrails to help them avoid having or creating problems. Ideally it will also connect them with a diverse and good-hearted network of peers and role models that will support a long, healthy lifetime within that place. 

I’ve developed and iterated onboarding programs and flows for diverse and extremely exacting clients over the past twenty years and distilled the following principles for a successful onboarding: 
## Fourteen onboarding patterns

The following patterns are solutions to typical problems that occur when we attempt to integrate a person into a culture or place that is new to them. 

1. **Necessary myths.** A new person usually has no clue about how people experience meaning in a new place. Therefore, during Onboarding, someone needs to tell the new person the origin stories and 1 or 2 collectively cherished myths about the place. 
2. **Just-enough.** A new person is already overwhelmed by the new place itself; there is a lot of detail to take in. So, a good Onboarding is on the minimalistic side. It’s not about providing comprehensive content about every old thing. It’s pragmatic, giving them the  absolute basics that are required to operate — and, instructions on how they can find out more about anything they need to know about. 
3. **Festive personal welcome.** A sound Onboarding includes a moment where the newcomer feels important, seen, and personally welcomed with a festive object or experience that embodies the culture. Like flowers.  
4. **Just-in-time.** Even though they are new, a person wants to feel a sense of agency as they move through the world. Timing matters a lot; the new person should receive signposting and  minimum viable content, invitations, and instructions just before they need them, so they don’t have to ask for them, so they feel secure, oriented, prepared.  
5. **Absolute no’s.** We can’t assume anyone knows the ground rules, and we want to protect people from falling on their face. Most newbies are very worried about screwing up, and want assurance that they won’t break anything, or break themselves. Any ten commandments, unbreakable rules, absolute no’s, strongly held norms must be covered in Onboarding. Rationale should be explained, as well as processes and consequences 
6. **Give them a checklist.** When navigating a new place, some people’ll want to just dive in and mess around — but who knows if they’ll mess around with the right things first? Others people can feel lost, and confused about what to focus on when they have free time, and just feel stuck. So, both types get a feeling of order, clarity, and direction when they are able to see a clear, minimal list of tasks they can check off. 
7. **Visible noob.** If nobody knows who’s the noob, a huge opportunity for learning and network-building is missed. Ensure that the new person is marked so that everyone else in the community can identify them and provide welcome and help. Provide guidance and expectations so the not-new people know how to productively interact with the noob. 
8. **Visible guides.** The newbie probably won’t know who’s good at what, who’s nice, or who’s willing to help. Mark those people out and facilitate introductions. The goal is to connect them with people who embody the culture, who will be positive role models, and who have diverse perspectives and backgrounds. Even 15-minute Meet & Greets are helpful and no skin off anyone’s nose. 
9. **Establish habits.** During onboarding people are the most motivated to adapt in ways that will help them succeed. So it is THE main opportunity to help the newcomer establish 1 or 2 high-leverage, keystone habits that will help them belong. These would be things they always do in a certain way at a certain time that accumulate to success or good health, like doing a project review Friday afternoons, or setting a timer before a visit to Fone. 
10. **With a buddy.** When a person is new, they might not know who exactly to turn to with even a tiny question. Seeking information in groups or within ill-understood hierarchies can be nerve-wracking. Pair the newcomer with a buddy who was Onboarded not too long ago, who will give them a nice friendly welcome and answer their random questions. Lots more to say about the buddy role. 
11. **With a cohort.** It can be lonely being new. Place the newcomer in a cohort of other peer newcomers. A cohort can be as small as 3 or as large as 10 or even 12. When you convene the cohort, focus on the present (how they are feeling, what they are wondering about); de-emphasize status and past experiences. Keep it informal and don’t create reasons for competition or judginess. Give them a couple fun things to do together, like a scavenger hunt or a pizza meal. 
	1. **Scavenger Hunt** is a light way to encourage newcomers to quickly get acquainted with key features of a new place, that can also encourage teamwork and network-building.  
	2. **Pizza Dinner** is an inexpensive way to foster informal friendly relations between members of newcomer cohort as well as other people who are part of the place. 
12. **Project-based.** A good onboarding gives the learner a tiny, fun, low-stakes, interesting project to do that is relevant to what they came to this place to accomplish. This is a productive way to direct their energy and desire to connect and perform. The project should require them to visit key part of the place, gather critical information, meet key people, and create an artifact of some kind. 
13. **Rituals.** The success of onboarding depends partly on the quality of attention the newcomer brings. The structure of the onboarding should inspire the newcomer to approach the new place with respect, inviting them to bring their best, for instance by attending to attire, promptness, or aspects of their identity in a particular, more detailed than usual way. A secret handshake should not be ruled out. The practices should invite them into a ritual moment where attention is heightened and a meaningful connection between the person and the place can be sparked. 
14. **Always improving.** Places change all the time, and onboarding can go stale quickly. A good onboarding is always gathering feedback from participants to evolve and improve. 

## Thoughtstarter — Onboard Spaceship Earth

Lately I’ve been wondering if our whole societal approach to education is just way too convoluted. Industrialized. Heavy. Oppressive. What if we thought about it differently: as a basic onboarding program focused on orienting new souls to our Spaceship Earth? What would be a minimalistic, cohort-based, festive, welcoming, sociable onboarding for a child getting to know this planet during their first sixteen years of living on it?
