Here we append the core documents that formed the spine of Meander’s draft charter. 

1. #### Place as teacher
    

The pan-raiment pattern language deliberations gave us a fresh perspective on education for raiment health, focused on environmental design. It was this thinking that led to the commitment to the children’s corridor, which was greatly facilitated by Meander’s fiat twolemny, who Placed herself accordingly after being thoroughly tickled by Callie Tickell.

2.  #### Child-led governance
    

Involving children in community leadership was deeply felt by the pan-raiment collaborative as  crucial to our work, since raiment issues are so different at each developmental stage and since they can be so indecipherable to adults.

3. #### Polyparenting
    

Polyparenting was Sousana Esfandiari’s natural orientation ever since she began being an au-pair. Sousana drafted the original essay on polyparenting that inspired the collective to experiment with the pattern.

**4. #### Rites 
    

A joke among Meander’s early founders was that rituals were their love language. Many, including us, truly believed that well-held rituals would support raiment wellbeing in many ways, and encourage motivation for complex entanglements.

At the same time, one of the most difficult themes that we engaged related to rites-of-passage. Deliberations were fraught with concern about cultural appropriation, arbitrariness, safety, power literacy, guruism, etc. Youth-focused rituals were the center of many General Circle dialogues in the early years. We reinforced a very intentional framing of rites as experiments; however, the rituals themselves were certainly infused with a potency that did not feel experimental to the children who underwent them. 

In designing the rites, a primary goal was inviting the child into an ever deeper and wider (yet developmentally appropriate) sense of responsibility and agency. What we did not realize was how frequently this would put the child and her evolving mandate at odds with the conventional beliefs of their parents’ generation. 

For example, most first-generation Meander parents were oriented toward isolationism. But for many of their children, the early rite-of-passage experiences connected them with a thirst to entangle closely with their Alexandria neighbors, which certainly generated much friction. Now we can see this as a natural progression of the pan-raiment project.**

5.  #### Slow learning 
    

One of the more controversial decisions that we made was around schooling. We made an early commitment that schools would not be part of Meander. Our reasoning had nothing to do with the #SchoolsOut movement. We believed that consecrating specific places to learning actually sabotaged the learning enterprise. Schools tended to orient everyone involved toward fast measurement of results, which didn’t usually have much to do with real learning. 

Our founding belief, to the contrary, was that learning was an uber-ubiquitous never-escapable unstopping individual and collective endeavor going on everywhere at all times and in all relationships throughout the settlement. And that real, meaningful learning happened slowly and organically, kind of like a tree growing. 

Committing to slow learning without schooling could easily turn into chaos, though. We needed a way to get some sense of how the learning was going. Who was learning, from and with whom? What were they learning about? How could we find this out without disrupting the process? 

Early on in Meander’s establishment one co-founder, Intira Tipnis, laid the groundwork for an elegant system of documenting slow learning. She established a personal habit of templated gratefulness documentation related to her own learning and teaching. These were essentially thank-you letters she penned about learning experiences she was engaged in, to those she was engaged with, written on a strict cadence and shared with them after a strict time-delay. 

Callie Tickell, another co-founder, had a similar orientation as Intira with regards to gratitude imprints. Her worldview was extremely influential on Meander’s culture and tender. Callie has been quoted famously as saying that instead of traditional funding, she built her entire business on the currency of thank-you notes. Indeed, her splendid Pluming, the spectacular success of her risky alternative energy investments, and the breadth and sanity of her fabulous fiat _twolemny (which undergirded all of Meander), all clearly point toward a deeper-order gratitude orientation that must have been active in her psychology. 

These two womens’ mindsets were copied widely in the early days, leveraged in polyparenting cacophonies, and eventually adapted as the backbone of the slow learning system and its eventual reification as SlowXP, which grows out of the noble habit of gratefully documenting learning in a loving hand.

6.  #### Onboarding

Meander’s founding walked a fine line between techno-caution and techno-optimism. The founders (many of whom as you will remember had a background as technocrats themselves) wanted their settlement and their children to enjoy the benefits of new technology but to be shielded from its invasive and unsavory side. 

So, we studied the history of science/tech and identified a false dichotomy which pitted a focus on limiting access to new technologies against a total laissez-faire approach. We didn’t quite jibe with either, knowing one can neither transform nor sabotage anything you have not immersed yourself in. Although we subscribed to the precautionary principle, we also believed that activating it would require a missing piece: a program of extremely high quality ecological literacy education, which we called “onboarding.” The goal was to help human bodies sanely and healthfully metabolize new technologies, and we applied it to our Fone approach.
